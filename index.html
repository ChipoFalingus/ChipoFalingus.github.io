#6 The Colony | 11/06/2026

It's been a minute! I've been spending some time cleaning up code and figuring out how to implement various gameplay mechanics. While those are getting sorted out, I figured I should talk about how mechanics that surround colony development.

Starting Out

Upon creating a new game, the player will be presented with a minimap of the world. They will get to choose where they want to start their colony. The player will be able to see the terrain and any structures that are present in the world. New colonies will start with a few colonists and a small amount of resources, including food, some wood, and a weapon + ammo.

The Border

Colonies, by default, will have a border surrounding the center. This border will determine the area that the player can build in. Harvesting resources outside the border will be possible, but it will have a restricted range (It's 100 tiles out right now, but that is subject to change). Players can expand their border by building structures close to the border, which will naturally expand it. The border mainly exists for performance reasons, but it also helps to keep player colonies more contained and manageable. The border will also be a visual representation of the colony's influence in the world.

Food

Food is the most important resource in the game to manage. No food means unhappy/unenergetic colonists, which means no work gets done. The starting food supply will last a day or two, so players will need to quickly find something else. Unfortunately, there are no animals in this game (at least not yet), so players will heavily rely on plants for food. I'll maybe add fishing into the first version to help offset this. Players will be required to find seeds and plant them to grow food. Grown products can usually be eaten right away, but players can also cook them into more filling meals.

Rooms

Rooms are created by surrounding an area with walls and a door. Rooms will be assigned a type based on the objects that are placed inside of it. If a room has a bed, it will be assigned as a bedroom. If it has a table and chairs, it will be assigned as a dining room. What's the purpose? Colonists will get upset if they have to sleep or eat outside. Rooms also provide protection from zombies. Colonists will also recognize rooms as safe spaces and will prioritize moving into them should they be in danger.

Defense

Players will have to defend their colony from zombies. Zombies will spawn in the world and will attempt to attack the colony. The starting weapon is a pistol, which can hold off a couple of zombies, but players will need to find metal resources to craft better weapons and armor. Players will also be able to build walls to keep zombies out, but they will need to be careful as zombies can break down walls. Guard posts can be built and colonists can be assigned as guards to be stationed or follow patrol routes.

So that's a general overview of some basic mechanics. I have a lot more ideas for the colony, but for now I want to focus on the basic stuff and get it working. These are the core features that will be present in the first version of the game and are the foundation for future stuff, so I want to make sure they are solid before moving on.

#5 The World | 09/29/2026

Time to talk about the actual foundation of the game, the world generation! The world generation for this game is in an early state right now and is not super fleshed out, so I'll cover ideas I have and some general game design on how to build an interesting and fun world.

How Does One Generate a World?

Procedural generation is not as scary as a topic as most people might think. It's built on top of deterministic math operations. The math behind it is complicated and I'm not going to act like I understand it super well, so I'll cover the general idea. The world generation is built of a combination of Voronoi Noise and Perlin Noise. Voronoi Noise is actually pretty simple, it's a bunch of scattered dots and every position on the grid is attached to the closest dot. This creates cell-like patterns that determine patches of land. Then I use Perlin Noise (which is a lot more complicated) and layer that on top of the Voronoi pattern to make the altitudes more varied. I also use a slight jitter on the edges to make the shape more interesting. Making an interesting world is a matter of combining several layers of noise to create something unique. All the items (trees, rocks, etc.) use similar methods to determine placement.

What Does Deterministic Mean?

I don't know if you were wondering, but it's still worth covering. Algorithms/Operations are considered deterministic if the same input ALWAYS returns the same output. There is zero randomness. This is how seeds work. If you've played a game that uses procedural generation, you're definitely familiar with seed values. Because the generation is deterministic, the same seed ALWAYS returns the same output. The only random part about world generation is figuring out what seed to use upon creation.

What's NOT Added In Yet

The world generation creates cool looking islands, but they are really plain and uninteresting. There is little variation from one seed to the next. The next step will be adding in structures. Because I'm going for a zombie apocalypse type of thing, I want to add abandoned structures and roads. The area I'm working with is relatively small, so I probably couldn't go with huge city centers or something like that. That does make me wonder if it's possible to fully explore the island in very little time and have literally nothing else to see, but that will be an issue for future me. I'll probably also add in a fog mechanic where unexplored chunks cannot be seen at all until a colonist walks into it, but I haven't fully decided on that yet.

I also was thinking about creating a second layer for caves. Right now the metal resources in the game are spread out across the map in patches exactly like in Factorio. Adding a second layer will require large rework of all the systems I've already put in place since they only work in a 2D setting. That being said, it could be a worthwhile investment should I ever commit to it.

Last but not least, I want the world to be a product of the simulation. The random structures and items you find should feel intentional and not randomly placed down. There should be a storytelling element for anything you find in the world. This is something I want to add in, but this will wait until far into the future when I have the groundwork all laid out.

So there you go! Everything here is incredibly ambitious, but I feel it's all necessary for the game I want.

#4 Spatial Querying | 09/16/2026

The previous blog post went over ECS architecture, which is super useful for a project like this. But there is a big problem, a REALLY big problem with it. ECS stores entities in what's called a registry, which is essentially a big list of entities. The entities are not sorted in any way, they are just added to the end of the list.

Why This is a Problem

Every object in the registry has an attached "Position" component which lists what coordinates that object is at. It's super easy retrieving the coordinates for a given object, but what about the other way around? The game rendering loop needs to check if there's an item on a given tile so that it knows what character to put there. The game also contains several object search algorithms that need to know what objects are close to a given coordinate. How can I find what object(s) lie on a given coordinate, if any? I could loop through the entire registry to find that exact coordinate I'm looking for, but the registry is tens, even hundreds of thousands of units long. And I would need to loop through it for EVERY SINGLE TILE ON SCREEN. So what to do...

Hash Maps + Registry Hybrid

This might get a little confusing if you're unfamiliar with hash maps, but I'll do my best to explain. A hash map is sort of like a list, but the way you access elements is with a key. You input a key, and get its corresponding element. The main appeal of this data structure is that accessing any element you want is really fast (O(1) time complexity if you care). We can use this to store items at given coordinates. The element we want to store is a list of items on a tile, and the key will be the coordinates of those items. Now I can retrieve items quickly by inputing their coordinates. The registry remains untouched, as it is still the way to access information about a given object. The only downside is adding or removing objects is a little finicky. Objects need to be added/removed from both the hash map and registry, or else it will exist as a ghost object in memory and take away precious performance. But besides that, this is a huge performance boost and doesn't even cost that much memory to store.

#3 ECS Architecture | 09/08/2026

Hello! I'm already really inconsistent with my blog posts. Anyway, I figured I should now talk about the backend architecture of the game, and how I used to be naive.

Let me break it down:

I am making a game that has a lot of different things in it. All of those things have vastly different functionality and behavior. I need a way to make certain items hold certain properties. The OLD way was a standard OOP (Object-Oriented Programming) approach. Say I wanted to add a new plant into the game. I want this plant to grow, I want it to be harvestable, and I want it to attack when someone tries to pick it up. Fun! I would need to write code inside that class for all of these behaviors, which is doable and simple enough. But what if I wanted to add a new plant that does all of that AND it can produce berries? Well, I'd need to create a brand new class for that plant, copy all of the code, and then add new functionality. This approach scales terribly. I'll end up with tons of classes that all share copied code. Even worse, storing all of these different objects is a heirarchy nightmare.

ECS fixes everything

ECS stands for Entity-Component-System. It's a design pattern that is used in game development to help organize code and data. I'm going to simplify this for the sake of explanation. This approach uses something called entities. What you can do with entities is attach components to them. Components are just blocks of functionality. For example, I can write a component called "Harvestable", which tells the game that this entity can be harvested. I can also write a component called "Growable", which says that this entity can grow like a plant. This fixes all of the issues I listed above. Back to the plant example, I can create components "Harvestable", "Growable", and "Attackable" and then attach them to a plant entity. Now if I want it to produce berries, I write the new component and just attach it, and it works. All components are completely reusable and can be attached to anything. Want colonists to emit light? Sure! Walls can be eaten? Yeah! It's a very flexible system that makes adding new items and functionality incredibly easy.

TL;DR: ECS is perfect for this game and OOP can burn in a fire.

#2 The Tech Stack | 08/26/2026

One of the most important decisions in development is choosing what tools to use. For this project, I am using C++ and a custom-built renderer using OpenGL. My IDE of choice is Visual Studio 2022. I am also using the FreeType library for OpenGL to render the text. Why am I not using a game engine like Unity or Godot? Because that's no fun! But seriously, I chose these tools for the learning opportunity. Also because I'm making a simulator game, every extra bit of performance I can squeeze out will go a long way into making a detailed world simulation.

#1 What Is This Game? | 08/15/2026

Before showcasing any features or diving into any behind-the-scenes stuff, I figured I should describe what this game even is and what to expect from it. This game is an ASCII-based colony simulator inspired by genre-defining titles like Dwarf Fortress and RimWorld.

What makes this one different?

I want my game to blend together colony building and RTS elements. You're tasked with standard colony game stuff, like keeping your colonists happy and fed. You also must defend your colony from rampant swarms of zombies that will attempt to destroy your colony. Give weapons to your colonists and build walls to keep zombies out. I also want to add an exploration mechanic where you can send a squad of your colonists to loot procedurally-generated locations.

Why a colony simulator?

Making a colony simulator seemed like a fun and interesting project. I've watched videos documenting the development of the other games mentioned and became interested in building an unpredictable simulation. It's fun adding new rules and watching them interact with the rest of the system in interesting ways.

Why ASCII?

Using ASCII graphics for the game speeds up development as I'm not being slowed down by visual fidelity. There's also an aesthetic to the simplicity of colored characters that I like. I have plans for graphics upgrades and/or custom ASCII characters down the line, but that will be a while from now.

I'll dive into more technical details starting next post, but this should give a better idea of what the end goal is and what I'm trying to accomplish.
